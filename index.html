1. The subatomic particles

Lesson 1 · LO 1(a), 1(c)
Essential ideaAll atoms are made of the same three particles in different amounts. The protons and neutrons (nucleons) sit in a very small nucleus; the electrons occupy the region around it. Almost all the mass is in the nucleus; almost all the volume is the electron region.

What you need to know

propertyelectronprotonneutron
relative charge−1+10
relative mass1/183611
locationregion around nucleusnucleusnucleus

Actual values (Data Booklet): charge ±1.60 × 10−19 C; mass of proton/neutron 1.67 × 10−27 kg, electron 9.11 × 10−31 kg. Nucleus diameter ≈ 10−13 m; atom ≈ 10−10 m.

Common trap"Nucleon number" is not the number of protons. It is protons + neutrons. The identity of an element is fixed by the number of protons only.

2. Chemical symbols: counting protons, neutrons and electrons

Lesson 1 · LO 1(d), 1(e)(i)
Essential ideaIn AZX, the subscript Z (proton number / atomic number) counts protons; the superscript A (nucleon number / mass number) counts protons + neutrons. Charge tells you how the electron count differs from the proton count.

What you need to know

  • protons = Z
  • neutrons = A − Z
  • electrons = Z for a neutral atom; Z − n for a cation Xn+; Z + n for an anion Xn−
  • Example: 3517Cl has 17 p, 18 n, 17 e; 3517Cl− has 18 e; 2713Al3+ has 10 e.

Worked example (Exercise 1.1)

Step 1 · What is asked
Give p, e, n for 2814Si, 7031Ga3+ and 3115P3−.
Step 2 · Protons from the subscript
Si: 14 · Ga: 31 · P: 15
Step 3 · Electrons from the charge
Si (neutral): 14 · Ga3+: 31 − 3 = 28 · P3−: 15 + 3 = 18
Step 4 · Neutrons = A − Z
Si: 28 − 14 = 14 · Ga: 70 − 31 = 39 · P: 31 − 15 = 16
Step 5 · Why
A positive ion has fewer electrons than protons; a negative ion has more. Neutrons never change with charge.

Try it: atom counter

Predict first: if the nucleon number changes but the proton number stays the same, does the element change?

TakeawayChanging A changes only the neutrons (a different isotope). Only Z fixes the element.

Your turn

Common trapMixing up which number is which. Subscript (bottom) = protons; superscript (top) = nucleons. And remember: an ion's charge changes the electrons, never the protons or neutrons.

3. Isotopes

Lesson 1 · LO 1(e)(ii)
Definition (learn it word for word)Isotopes are atoms of an element that have the same number of protons but different number of neutrons (same proton number, different nucleon number).

What you need to know

  • Common isotopes: 1H, 2H, 3H; 12C, 13C; 14N, 15N; 16O, 18O; 32S, 34S; 35Cl, 37Cl. Deuterium is written 21H.
  • Isotopes have different relative isotopic masses and physical properties (e.g. behaviour in an electric field).
  • Isotopes have similar chemical properties because they have the same number of electrons.
  • Isotopes may be stable or unstable (radioactive), e.g. 14C for carbon dating.

Your turn

Common trapConfusing "different isotopes" with "different ions". Isotopes differ in neutrons; ions of the same element differ in electrons. A question can combine both (same protons, different neutrons and different electrons).

Quick recap

4. Relative atomic mass and relative isotopic mass

Lesson 1 · LO 5(a) part, 5(c)
Definitions Relative atomic mass, Ar = (average mass of one atom of an element) ÷ (1/12 × the mass of one atom of 12C).
Relative isotopic mass = (mass of one atom of an isotope of an element) ÷ (1/12 × the mass of one atom of 12C).
The word average is essential in the Ar definition because it takes the isotopes into account; it is irrelevant for an isotope.

What you need to know

Ar = Σ (relative isotopic mass × isotopic abundance). For chlorine (35Cl : 37Cl = 3 : 1): Ar = (35 × 0.75) + (37 × 0.25) = 26.25 + 9.25 = 35.5.

Worked example (Exercise 1.4 Q1 — krypton)

Step 1 · What is asked
Ar to 1 d.p. from % abundances: 80 (2%), 82 (12%), 83 (12%), 84 (57%), 86 (17%).
Step 2 · Method
Ar = Σ(mass × abundance). Because abundances are percentages, divide by 100.
Step 3 · Calculation
Ar = (80×2 + 82×12 + 83×12 + 84×57 + 86×17) / 100 = 8388/100 = 83.9
Step 4 · Trap
If abundances are given as peak heights or ratios (not %), divide by the total of the heights, not by 100. See the lead question below.

Your turn

Common trap(1) Dividing by 100 when the abundances are peak heights that do not add to 100. (2) In the definition, writing "mass of one atom" without "average", or comparing with "one mole of 12C" instead of "one atom of 12C" (see Exercise 1.4 Q3).

5. Behaviour of particles in an electric field

Lesson 1 · LO 1(b)
Essential ideaProtons (and cations) are deflected towards the negative plate; electrons (and anions) towards the positive plate; neutrons are not deflected. For particles at the same speed, the angle of deflection θ ∝ q/m (charge ÷ mass).

What you need to know

  • Electrons are deflected far more than protons because they are much lighter (same size of charge, mass 1/1836).
  • Use a reference particle given in the question and proportion: θnew = θref × (q/m)new ÷ (q/m)ref.
  • Keep the sign: opposite charge → opposite direction.

Worked example (Exercise 2.1, N12/III/4a)

Step 1 · What is asked
Protons deflect +15°. Find the angles for D− (2H−), T+ (3H+) and He2+.
Step 2 · Relevant information
Reference: 1H+, q/m = +1/1 ↔ +15°.
Step 3 · Method
θ ∝ q/m. Tabulate q/m for each species, then scale.
Step 4 · Work
D−: q/m = −1/2 → θ = −½ × 15 = −7.5°
T+: q/m = +1/3 → θ = ⅓ × 15 = +5.0°
He2+: q/m = +2/4 = +1/2 → θ = +7.5°
Step 5 · Why / trap
D− and He2+ have the same size of q/m but opposite signs, so they deflect by the same angle in opposite directions. Always consider both charge and mass.

Try it: deflection simulator

Predict first: which deflects more, 63Cu2+ or 127I−? In which directions?

TakeawayThe sign of the charge sets the direction (towards the oppositely charged plate); the size of q/m sets the angle. Very large angles are capped in the drawing for the electron.

Your turn

Common trapComparing charge only ("2+ deflects more than 1+") and forgetting mass. 4He2+ (q/m = ½) deflects half as much as 1H+ (q/m = 1). Also: a neutral atom (e.g. a hydrogen atom) is not deflected at all.

6. Shells, subshells and orbitals

Lesson 2 · LO 1(f), 1(g)
DefinitionAn atomic orbital is the region of space with at least 90% probability of finding an electron. Electrons do not move in fixed orbits or paths.

What you need to know

nsubshellsorbitalsmax e− (2n2)
11s12
22s 2p1 + 3 = 48
33s 3p 3d1 + 3 + 5 = 918
44s 4p (4d 4f)1432

s: one orbital (2 e−); p: three orbitals (6 e−); d: five orbitals (10 e−). Each orbital holds a maximum of 2 electrons. Only s and p shapes are in the syllabus; 4d and 4f are not.

  • Orbitals in the same subshell are degenerate (same energy) but differ in orientation.
  • Within a shell, energy order of subshells: s < p < d < f. 2p is slightly higher than 2s, so a 2p electron is slightly further from the nucleus.
  • Higher n → further from nucleus, higher energy, less strongly attracted.
  • 4s is lower in energy than 3d, so the 19th and 20th electrons go into 4s.
  • s orbitals: spherical; size increases 1s < 2s < 3s. p orbitals: dumb-bell shaped, nucleus between two lobes (node at nucleus), three mutually perpendicular orbitals px, py, pz; size 2p < 3p < 4p. No p orbitals in shell 1.

Observe: the s orbital is a sphere around the nucleus; each p orbital has two lobes along one axis. Predict: how many orbitals does the 2p subshell contain?

TakeawayThree p orbitals per p subshell (answer: 3), identical in size and energy, mutually perpendicular. Shading is not required when you draw them; draw the axes to show 3D.

Your turn

Common trap"An electron in a p orbital follows a dumb-bell shaped path" — wrong. The orbital is a region of probability, not a path. Also wrong: "all electrons with the same n have the same energy" (2s is slightly lower than 2p).

7. Electronic configuration

Lesson 2 · LO 1(h)
The three rules Aufbau: fill the lowest-energy orbital first (1s, 2s, 2p, 3s, 3p, 4s, 3d, 4p…).
Hund's rule: degenerate orbitals are filled singly with parallel spins before pairing (to avoid inter-electronic repulsion).
Pauli: an orbital holds at most 2 electrons, with opposite spins.

What you need to know

  • The lowest-energy configuration is the ground state; any other is an excited state.
  • Exceptions: Cr is 1s22s22p63s23p63d54s1 (not 3d44s2); Cu is …3d104s1 (not 3d94s2).
  • Cations: write the atom, then remove electrons from the outermost (highest energy) subshell — 4s electrons are removed before 3d. Cu2+: …3p63d9; Fe2+: …3p63d6.
  • Anions: write the atom, then add electrons to the lowest-energy orbitals available. S2−: 1s22s22p63s23p6.
  • Do not use noble-gas cores like [Ar] when asked to write configurations in examinations.

Try it: configuration builder

Predict first: for Fe2+, are the two electrons removed from 3d or 4s?

Takeaway4s fills before 3d (lower energy), but 4s electrons are removed first when a cation forms. Fe2+ loses both 4s electrons: …3d6. Unpaired electrons are counted from the box diagram.

Worked example (Exercise 3.3)

Step 1 · What is asked
Configurations of 11Na+, 26Fe2+, 17Cl−.
Step 2 · Atom first
Na: 1s22s22p63s1 · Fe: 1s22s22p63s23p63d64s2 · Cl: 1s22s22p63s23p5
Step 3 · Remove / add from the highest-energy subshell
Na+: remove the 3s electron → 1s22s22p6
Fe2+: remove the two 4s electrons → 1s22s22p63s23p63d6
Cl−: add one electron to 3p → 1s22s22p63s23p6
Step 4 · Trap
Electrons are not removed in the reverse order of filling: 4s fills before 3d, but 4s is also emptied first (Exercise 3.6 statement 7 is wrong).

Your turn

Common traps (Exercise 3.6)Wrong statements: "an electron in a p orbital follows a dumb-bell path"; "all electrons with the same principal quantum number have the same energy"; "electrons always occupy px first"; "electrons are removed in the reverse order of filling". Correct: an s orbital holds max 2 e−; electrons in the same subshell have the same energy; lowest energy orbitals fill first.

8. Electronic configuration and the Periodic Table

Lesson 2 · LO 4(a)
Essential ideaElements are arranged by proton number. Elements in the same group have the same number of outer (valence) electrons and similar valence-shell configurations. Elements in the same period have their outer electrons in the same outermost shell.

What you need to know

Group12131415161718
Period 2Li [He]2s1Be 2s2B 2s22p1C 2s22p2N 2s22p3O 2s22p4F 2s22p5Ne 2s22p6
Period 3Na [Ne]3s1Mg 3s2Al 3s23p1Si 3s23p2P 3s23p3S 3s23p4Cl 3s23p5Ar 3s23p6
  • Period number = the outermost occupied shell (highest n).
  • Group number: count valence electrons (Group 1–2: s electrons; Groups 13–18: 10 + s + p electrons).
  • Blocks: s-block (last electron into s), p-block (into p), d-block (transition elements, e.g. Cu, Fe, Ni — different properties).
  • Example (Exercise 3.7): Z = 33 → 1s22s22p63s23p63d104s24p3 → Period 4, Group 15.

Your turn (Checkpoint 2 Q1)

Common trapReading the group of a p-block element as just the number of p electrons. Group = 10 + (s + p valence electrons) for Groups 13–18, e.g. 3s23p3 → Group 15.

9. Ionisation energy and the factors that affect it

Lesson 3 · LO 1(i)
Definitions (word for word) First ionisation energy is the energy needed to remove 1 mole of electrons from 1 mole of gaseous atoms to form 1 mole of singly charged gaseous cations: X(g) → X+(g) + e−
Second ionisation energy is the energy required to remove 1 mole of electrons from 1 mole of singly charged gaseous cations to form 1 mole of doubly charged gaseous cations: X+(g) → X2+(g) + e−
State symbols (g) are compulsory — a different state would need a different amount of energy. Use a full-headed arrow. Example: Na(g) → Na+(g) + e−, +494 kJ mol−1; Na+(g) → Na2+(g) + e−, +4560 kJ mol−1.

The three factors — how strongly is the electron attracted by the nucleus?

FactorEffect
Number of quantum shellsMore shells → electron further from nucleus → weaker attraction → lower IE
Nuclear chargeMore protons → greater nuclear charge → stronger attraction → higher IE
Shielding (screening) effectInner-shell electrons repel the outer electron. More inner electrons → greater shielding → weaker attraction → lower IE
  • Effective nuclear charge, Zeff ≈ number of protons − number of inner electrons. It is the net nuclear charge the outer electron experiences after shielding.
  • Electrons in the same shell/subshell shield each other only slightly (2p is slightly shielded by 2s). Shielding ability within a shell: s > p > d > f. Electrons in the same orbital provide a little shielding due to inter-electronic repulsion.

Your turn

Common trap (GST)Equations without state symbols or with a half-headed arrow lose the mark. Also: "chlorine has one more proton" is not the same as "chlorine has a higher nuclear charge" — the marker should not have to interpret your words. Use the terms nuclear charge, shielding effect, effective nuclear charge.

10. Successive ionisation energies

Lesson 3 · LO 1(j), 1(k)
Essential ideaRemoving electrons one after another from the same atom gives successive IEs that generally increase, with a large jump each time the next electron comes from an inner quantum shell and a small increase when it comes from a different subshell that is closer to the nucleus.

What you need to know (magnesium, 1s22s22p63s2)

  • After each electron is removed, nuclear charge remains the same, shielding by remaining electrons decreases, so Zeff increases and the next electron needs more energy (2nd IE > 1st IE).
  • 3rd electron comes from 2p, an inner quantum shell much closer to the nucleus → much stronger attraction → large increase.
  • 9th electron comes from 2s, a subshell slightly closer than 2p → small increase.
  • 11th electron comes from 1s, the next inner shell → large increase again.
  • First large jump → number of valence electrons (so the Group). Number of large jumps → number of quantum shells.

Sketch the successive IEs (qualitative, not to scale)

Predict first: for Na (1s22s22p63s1), after which electron does the first large jump occur?

TakeawayCount the electrons before the first big jump = Group (valence electrons). Count the big jumps = number of shells. The sketch is drawn from the configuration (as in Figure 4.1 of your notes); it is not real data.

Worked example (Exercise 4.3)

Step 1 · What is asked
IEs / kJ mol−1: 870, 1800, 3000, 3600, 5800, 7000, 13200. Which element: 33As, 40Zr, 52Te, 53I?
Step 2 · Look for the first big jump
7000 → 13200 (between the 6th and 7th electron) is the biggest increase, signalling a change of quantum shell.
Step 3 · Deduce
6 electrons in the outermost shell → Group 16.
Step 4 · Answer
Of the options, only Te is in Group 16 → C.

Your turn (Exercise 4.2)

Common trap (GST Q4)When explaining a general increase in successive IE, say that nuclear charge remains the same and shielding decreases (not "nuclear charge increases"). For the big jump, say the electron is removed from an inner quantum shell much closer to the nucleus, so it is more strongly attracted.

11. Trends in first ionisation energy

Lesson 3 · LO 4(b)
Down a group (e.g. Li → Cs)Nuclear charge increases and shielding increases → effective nuclear charge roughly the same. Number of quantum shells increases → outermost electron further from nucleus → less strongly attracted → 1st IE decreases. (Li 519, Na 494, K 418, Rb 403, Cs 376 kJ mol−1)
Across a period (e.g. Na → Ar)Nuclear charge increases (more protons). Shielding remains relatively constant (added electrons go into the same outermost shell; inner electrons unchanged). → Effective nuclear charge increases → outermost electron more strongly attracted → 1st IE generally increases.

Observe: the general rise across Period 3 and the two dips. Predict: which two pairs break the trend?

TakeawayDips at Mg → Al and P → S. Each has its own specific reason (below). Learn the Data Booklet values when asked to "quote values".

The two dips in Period 3

Group 2 → 13: Al (577) lower than Mg (736)Mg: 1s22s22p63s2; Al: …3s23p1. The 3p electron in Al is further away from the nucleus than the 3s electron in Mg, so it is less strongly attracted and less energy is needed to remove it.
Group 15 → 16: S (1000) lower than P (1060)P: …3p3 (three unpaired); S: …3p4 (one pair). The paired 3p electrons in S experience inter-electronic repulsion, so less energy than expected is needed to remove one of them.

12. Trends in atomic radius and ionic radius

Lesson 4 · LO 4(b)
Essential ideaThe same three factors explain radius. Stronger attraction between the nucleus and the outermost electrons pulls them closer → smaller radius.
Atomic radius down a groupNuclear charge and shielding both increase → Zeff roughly the same. Number of quantum shells increases → outermost electrons further away → radius increases.
Atomic radius across Period 2/3Nuclear charge increases; shielding relatively constant (same inner shells) → Zeff increases → outermost electrons pulled closer → radius decreases. (S 0.104 nm, Cl 0.099 nm)
Ionic radius across Period 3 Cations: Na+ > Mg2+ > Al3+ > Si4+ (all 10 electrons). Anions: P3− > S2− > Cl− (all 18 electrons).
Within each isoelectronic series (same number of electrons): nuclear charge increases while shielding stays constant (same electrons) → Zeff increases → ionic radius decreases.
From Si4+ to P3−: sharp increase because the anions have one more quantum shell than the cations.

Note: the radii for He, Ne, Ar in the Data Booklet are van der Waals' radii (always larger than covalent radii), which is why the noble gases look anomalously large.

Worked example (GST Q1 extension)

Step 1 · What is asked
Explain why a P atom is smaller than a Na atom, but P3− is larger than Na+. [3]
Step 2 · Atoms: same period
P has a higher nuclear charge and similar shielding effect to Na. Effective nuclear charge of P is higher.
Step 3 · Link to the property
Stronger electrostatic attraction between the nucleus and the outermost electron in P → P has a smaller atomic radius than Na.
Step 4 · Ions: different number of shells
P3− (18 e−, 3 shells) has one more quantum shell than Na+ (10 e−, 2 shells).
Step 5 · Link
Weaker attraction between the nucleus and the remaining electrons in P3− → P3− has a larger ionic radius than Na+.

Your turn

Common trapComparing an anion with a cation using "nuclear charge" when the real difference is the number of quantum shells. Check the electron count first: isoelectronic → argue with nuclear charge; different shells → argue with shells.

13. How to write a full explanation (the marking chain)

Lessons 3–4 · from GST session
Success criteria
  1. Specific to the question: name the two species being compared; quote Data Booklet values with units when asked ("1st IE of Na: 494 kJ mol−1, P: 1060 kJ mol−1").
  2. Nuclear charge (higher / same / increases).
  3. Shielding effect (similar / relatively constant / increases / decreases).
  4. Effective nuclear charge (across a period / isoelectronic / successive IE) or number of quantum shells (down a group / anion vs cation).
  5. Link to the property: attraction between nucleus and outermost electron → energy required to remove it (IE) / distance of outermost electron (radius).

Model (GST Q2): "1st IE of Na: 494 kJ mol−1, 1st IE of P: 1060 kJ mol−1. P has a higher nuclear charge and similar shielding effect as Na. Effective nuclear charge of P is higher. More energy is required to overcome the stronger attraction between the outermost electron and the nucleus in P than in Na."

W1 · Ar from peak heights (Checkpoint 1 Q3b)

Lead: mass number 204, 206, 207, 208; peak heights 2.7, 48.0, 41.5, 92.6 (arbitrary units, proportional to abundance). Calculate Ar to 1 d.p.

Step 1 · What is asked
A weighted average of the isotopic masses. The heights are not percentages.
Step 2 · Relevant information
Total height = 2.7 + 48.0 + 41.5 + 92.6 = 184.8
Step 3 · Method
Ar = Σ(mass × height) ÷ total height
Step 4 · Work
(204 × 2.7 + 206 × 48.0 + 207 × 41.5 + 208 × 92.6) / 184.8 = 38 290.3 / 184.8 = 207.19…
Step 5 · Answer
Ar of the sample = 207.2 (1 d.p.)
Step 6 · Trap
Dividing by 100 gives 382.9 — an impossible Ar for lead. Always sanity-check: the answer must lie between the smallest and largest isotopic mass.

W2 · Deflection with a different reference (Checkpoint 1 Q4)

Separate beams of 63Cu2+ and 127I− pass through an electric field. The Cu2+ beam deflects 7.0°. Find the angle of the I− beam (1 d.p.) and describe the paths.

Step 1 · What is asked
Angle for I− and the directions of both beams.
Step 2 · Relevant information
θ ∝ q/m. Cu2+: q/m = 2/63. I−: q/m = 1/127 (size).
Step 3 · Set up the ratio
θ(I−) / θ(Cu2+) = (1/127) / (2/63)
Step 4 · Work
θ(I−) = (1/127) × (63/2) × 7.0 = 1.7° (1 d.p.)
Step 5 · Directions
Cu2+ is deflected towards the negative plate with the larger angle; I− towards the positive plate with a much smaller angle.

W3 · Configuration of a transition-metal cation (based on Ex 3.3 and Tutorial B Q7)

Write the electronic configuration of the iron cation in Fe(NO3)3.

Step 1 · What is asked
The ion is Fe3+ (three nitrate ions, each 1−).
Step 2 · Atom first
Fe (Z = 26): 1s22s22p63s23p63d64s2
Step 3 · Remove from the outermost (highest-energy) subshell first
Remove the two 4s electrons, then one 3d electron.
Step 4 · Answer
Fe3+: 1s22s22p63s23p63d5 (i.e. [Ar] 3d5 4s0) — a half-filled 3d subshell with 5 unpaired electrons.
Step 5 · Trap
Removing 3d electrons first (giving 3d34s2) is wrong. 4s fills first and empties first.

W4 · Identify an element from a second-IE graph (Tutorial B Q32, same pattern as GST Q3)

The 2nd IEs of 10 consecutive elements A–J (Z between 6 and 20) rise from A to a peak at D, fall sharply to E, then rise gently to J. Identify the Group of H and write its ground-state configuration. [2]

Step 1 · What is asked
Group of H and its configuration. We need to locate one element on the graph first.
Step 2 · Read the sharp drop
A sharp drop in 2nd IE from D to E means E+ loses its second electron from a shell further from the nucleus than D+ does. D+ has a noble-gas configuration → D is Group 1; E is Group 2.
Step 3 · Place the elements
D = Na (Z = 11), so E = Mg, F = Al, G = Si, H = P, I = S, J = Cl. (Check: A = O, B = F, C = Ne — the dip G below F matches Si+ (3p) vs Al+ (3s).)
Step 4 · Answer
H is in Group 15; configuration 1s22s22p63s23p3.
Step 5 · Why
For a 2nd IE graph, the element with the highest value in the run is Group 1 (its ion has a full shell). This is one element later than the 1st IE pattern, where the Group 18 element is the peak.

W5 · Explain a difference in atomic radius and get the marks (GST Q1)

Explain the difference in the atomic radii of a sulfur atom (0.104 nm) and a chlorine atom (0.099 nm). [2]

Step 1 · What is asked
A 2-mark explanation comparing two named species in the same period.
Step 2 · Identify the chain
Same period → use nuclear charge, shielding, effective nuclear charge, then link to the attraction on the outermost electron.
Step 3 · Model answer
"Chlorine has a higher nuclear charge than sulfur but a similar shielding effect. The effective nuclear charge of chlorine is higher, so there is a stronger attraction between the nucleus and the outermost electrons in chlorine. The outermost electrons are pulled closer, so chlorine has a smaller atomic radius than sulfur."
Step 4 · Compare with Response A (GST)
"Across the period, nuclear charge increases while shielding effect increases negligibly. There is stronger attraction … for chlorine, so chlorine has a smaller atomic radius." — accurate regurgitation but not specific: it never states the relative positions / compares S with Cl directly. Loses credit.
Step 5 · Compare with Response B (GST)
"…the chlorine atom has one more proton…" forces the marker to interpret this as "higher nuclear charge". Use the term nuclear charge explicitly.

W6 · Group from successive IEs, then compare with the next element (Tutorial B Q31)

Element X (Period 3): IE / kJ mol−1 = 1011, 1907, 2914, 4963, 6273, 21267, 25431, 29872. (a) Group of X? [1] (b) Y is in Period 3 with one more proton than X. Is the 1st IE of Y greater or smaller than that of X? Explain. [2]

Step 1 · First large jump
6273 → 21267 between the 5th and 6th electrons → 5 valence electrons → Group 15, X is phosphorus.
Step 2 · Identify Y
One more proton → Y is sulfur, 1s22s22p63s23p4.
Step 3 · Decide and explain
1st IE of Y (S) is smaller than that of X (P). The paired 3p electrons in S experience inter-electronic repulsion, so less energy is required to remove one of them than to remove an unpaired 3p electron from P.
Step 4 · Trap
Do not answer "greater, because nuclear charge increases". The general trend does not apply at Group 15 → 16.

W7 · Three elements, two comparisons (Tutorial B Q37, PrelimJJC17/III/1b)

Explain why the first ionisation energy of calcium is higher than that of potassium and gallium. [2]

Step 1 · Configurations
K: …4s1; Ca: …4s2; Ga: …3d104s24p1. All Period 4; the 1st electron removed is 4s (K, Ca) and 4p (Ga).
Step 2 · Ca vs K (same period, same subshell)
Ca has a higher nuclear charge and similar shielding effect to K → higher effective nuclear charge → stronger attraction between the nucleus and the 4s electron → more energy needed.
Step 3 · Ca vs Ga (the Group 2 → 13 pattern)
The 4p electron removed from Ga is further from the nucleus (higher energy) than the 4s electron removed from Ca, so it is less strongly attracted and less energy is needed — the same reason as Mg vs Al in Period 3.
Step 4 · Trap
Two different comparisons need two different reasons. Writing only "Ca has more protons than K" earns at most one mark.

Instructions: Each partner writes the answers on the board within 4 min, then check each other (2 min); reveal (2 min).

In a plasma, 1H and 4He nuclei are deflected towards plate A by 4° and 2° respectively. (i) Polarity of plate A? (ii) Why is 1H deflected twice as much as 4He? (iii) Angles (1 d.p.) for 2H nuclei and 3He nuclei?

(i) Plate A is negative: the nuclei are positively charged (1H+, 4He2+) and are attracted to the negative plate.
(ii) q/m for 1H+ = 1/1 = 1; for 4He2+ = 2/4 = ½. Angle ∝ q/m, so 1H+ is deflected twice as much.
(iii) 2H+: q/m = ½ → ½ × 4 = 2.0°. 3He2+: q/m = 2/3 → 2/3 × 4 = 2.7°.

Follow-up: a 3H nucleus? (1/3 × 4 = 1.3°.)

WB3.1 · Mark it like the marker (GST Q1) — 10 min

Instructions: Project the question and the two responses. Pairs award a mark out of 2 to each response and write one sentence justifying the mark (4 min). Then write their own 2-mark answer on the board (3 min). Reveal and compare with the success criteria (3 min).

Explain the difference in the atomic radii of a sulfur atom (0.104 nm) and a chlorine atom (0.099 nm). [2]

Response A: "Across the period, nuclear charge increases while shielding effect increases negligibly. There is stronger attraction between outermost electron and nucleus for chlorine, so chlorine has a smaller atomic radius than sulfur."

Response B: "Sulfur and chlorine have similar shielding effect, but the chlorine atom has one more proton. This results in a greater effective nuclear charge acting on the electrons for chlorine. The outermost electron of chlorine is attracted closer to the nucleus, so it has a smaller atomic radius than sulfur."

Response A — 1/2. Accurate but generic: it never compares the two given species (where are S and Cl relative to each other?). An explanation must be specific to the question.

Response B — ~2/2, at risk. The marker is forced to interpret "one more proton" as "higher nuclear charge". Say it explicitly.

Success criteria: specific to the species → nuclear charge → shielding effect → effective nuclear charge (same period) → link: attraction between outermost electron and nucleus → radius.

Follow-up (GST Q1 extension): Why is a P atom smaller than Na but P3− larger than Na+? (Atoms: higher nuclear charge, similar shielding → higher Zeff, stronger attraction. Ions: P3− has one more quantum shell → weaker attraction.)

WB3.2 · Build the reasoning: 2nd IE graph (GST Q3 pattern) — 12 min

Instructions: Elements A–G are consecutive. Their 2nd IEs rise from A to F, with a small dip from C to D, and fall sharply from F to G. Partner A writes the equation for the 2nd IE of G and identifies G. Partner B explains the general increase from A+ to F+. Together: explain the dip from C+ to D+ (6 min). Reveal and mark (6 min).

(i) G+(g) → G2+(g) + e− — state symbols and a full-headed arrow are required.

(ii) G is magnesium, Group 2. The sharp dip from F to G shows that the second electron in G is removed from a quantum shell further from the nucleus than in F. G+: [Ne]3s1; G: [Ne]3s2. (So F = Na, E = Ne, D = F, C = O, B = N, A = C.)

(iii) From A+ to F+, nuclear charge increases and shielding effect remains relatively constant. Effective nuclear charge increases and the attraction between the outermost electrons and the nucleus becomes stronger, so more energy is required to remove the electron from the singly charged ion.

(iv) C+: 1s22s22p3; D+: 1s22s22p4. The inter-electronic repulsion between the paired 2p electrons in D+ makes it easier to remove the electron than the unpaired 2p electron in C+.

Lesson 4

WB4.1 · Solve and compare: order of atomic radii (Checkpoint 3 Q7) — 10 min

Instructions: Arrange Cs, F, Na, Cl in order of increasing atomic radius and explain. Partner A writes the down-the-group argument; Partner B writes the across-the-period argument (5 min). Combine into one answer on the board (2 min). Reveal (3 min).

F < Cl < Na < Cs. Na and Cs are in Group 1; F and Cl in Group 17. Down a group, effective nuclear charge is roughly the same as nuclear charge and shielding both increase; the number of quantum shells increases, so the outermost electrons are further from the nucleus — Cs is larger than Na and Cl is larger than F. Na and Cl are in Period 3: across the period nuclear charge increases while shielding stays relatively constant, so effective nuclear charge increases; the outermost electrons are more strongly attracted and pulled closer — Cl is smaller than Na.

Follow-up: Which species has the largest radius: P3−, Cl−, Ar, K+? (P3−: isoelectronic series, lowest nuclear charge.)

WB4.2 · Spot the error: four flawed explanations — 10 min

Instructions: Each statement contains one error. Pairs identify and rewrite each (6 min). Reveal (4 min).

(1) "Cl− is smaller than S2− because Cl− has fewer electrons."
(2) "The 2nd IE of Mg is higher than the 1st because the nuclear charge increases after the first electron is removed."
(3) "Down Group 1, 1st IE decreases because the shielding effect increases, so effective nuclear charge decreases."
(4) "The 1st IE of Al is lower than Mg because Al has paired electrons which repel."

(1) They are isoelectronic (both 18 e−). Cl− has a higher nuclear charge with the same shielding → higher Zeff → electrons pulled closer.
(2) Nuclear charge remains the same; shielding by the remaining electrons decreases, so Zeff increases.
(3) Nuclear charge and shielding both increase, so Zeff is roughly the same; the decrease is due to the increasing number of quantum shells (outermost electron further away).
(4) The 3p electron in Al is further from the nucleus than the 3s electron in Mg. Paired-electron repulsion is the P → S reason, not Mg → Al.
